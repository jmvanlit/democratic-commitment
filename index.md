		<h2>The research project: Liberal Democratic Commitment in Europe: Mass Attitudes, Political Identity, and Behavioural Consequences</h2>
		<p>Liberal democracy is under pressure worldwide. Illiberal actors are gaining support for a platform that directly challenges some central liberal democratic principles. Where such actors gain access to government, they frequently proceed to dismantle executive and erode democracy. So why do citizens often fail to resist the simultaneous rise of illiberalism and democratic backsliding by upholding liberal democratic principles at the ballot box? Building on the findings from a <a href="https://data.snf.ch/grants/grant/185908">previous Ambizione project (2019-2024)</a>, this <a href = "https://data.snf.ch/grants/grant/10005358">new SNSF-funded project (2025-2029)</a> will investigate the relevance and heterogeneity of mass attitudes towards democracy as a fundamental factor explaining citizens’ responses to emerging threats to liberal democracy. <a href = "#two">The project team</a> will theorize and empirically explore variation in liberal democratic commitment as a way to resolve the apparent paradox between mass support for democracy and citizens’ differential willingness to tolerate or even openly endorse political actors who challenge core principles of liberal democracy.</p>

	<div class="inner">
		<h2>News and updates</h2>
			<ul class="news">
	<li><span class="news-date">13 October 2026</span> · <strong>N. Wunsch</strong> and S. E. Aytaç published a study on Hungarians' commitment to liberal democracy before, during, and after the 2026 general elections on SSRN. <a href="https://dx.doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7567361">Read more</a>
		<details class="abstract">
			<summary>Abstract</summary>
			<p>How does electoral turnover affect citizens' liberal democratic commitment? Supporting constraints on executive power costs little while one's own party is out of office, but electoral turnover offers a critical test of whether democratic priorities represent a stable normative orientation or are contingent on political power. In this paper, we leverage a real-world electoral turnover following Hungary's April 2026 parliamentary elections, in which the former opposition party Tisza won a supermajority after sixteen years of deepening autocratization under Fidesz rule. Combining a three-wave panel survey and experimental evidence around this election, we examine how winner-loser status affects the relative importance citizens attach to liberal and majoritarian understandings of democracy. We find substantial partisan differences before the election and systematic updating after turnover, whereby electoral losers become more liberal and winners more majoritarian. These shifts are meaningful but partial, suggesting that citizens' democratic priorities are partly contingent on political power rather than fixed principles. Liberal democratic commitment thus appears to weaken at the moment it would matter mostwhen a party has newly gained office. This finding has important implications for how we assess citizens' capacity to constrain governments in polarized democracies and speaks to broader debates about democratic resilience and restoration.</p>
		</details>
	</li>
	<li><span class="news-date">17 June 2026</span> · We have fielded 16 focus groups in Italy, Finland, Poland, and France to understand how citizens talk about their support for democracy.
	</li>
	<li><span class="news-date">15 May 2026</span> · <strong>N. Wunsch</strong>, <strong>J. van Lit</strong>, and T. Petrova published a study on Czechs' commitment to liberal democracy on OSF. <a href="https://osf.io/preprints/osf/eqnxs_v1">Read more</a>
		<details class="abstract">
			<summary>Abstract</summary>
			<p>How do elite justifications shape citizens’ responses to democratic transgressions? Existing studies emphasize partisan loyalty and policy trade-offs, but we know less about responses to deliberate attempts to justify antidemocratic actions. Using a within-subject vignette experiment fielded in Czechia (n = 2,040), we compare normative appeals to majoritarianism, affective appeals to partisan threat, and instrumental appeals to economic performance and stability. Citizens overwhelmingly recognize transgressions as undemocratic, yet majoritarian and especially stability appeals make them less critical. Contrary to expectations, economic appeals are weakly effective, while partisan threat produces a mild backlash. The justifications affect respondents’ likelihood of voting for their preferred party only moderately. Open-ended responses show that respondents engage with the justifications in the theorized terms, while many nevertheless ground their evaluations in principled democratic commitment. The study shows how citizens evaluate undemocratic proposals in light of elite justifications, and which of these they find most – and least – persuasive.</p>
		</details>
	</li>
